# Coaching Framework

## 1. Align
Clarify development goals, workplace context, roles, cadence, time commitment, information-sharing boundaries, and how progress will be reviewed. The participant, coach, and manager should understand what coaching is—and is not.

## 2. Establish a baseline
Use participant self-assessment, coach observations, and carefully scoped stakeholder input. Ask for concrete examples, not global judgments. Select 2–3 priority behaviors.

## 3. Practice
Each session includes reflection, review of the previous experiment, one focused model, a realistic exercise, feedback, a second attempt, and a small commitment.

## 4. Apply at work
Assignments should be relevant to normal work and low-risk: use a listening checklist, rewrite a message before sending, or present a proposal using BLUF.

## 5. Review and sustain
Repeat the competency assessment, discuss evidence, collect proportionate endline feedback, and agree on a 30/60/90-day continuation plan.

## First conversation
"You bring valuable technical expertise. This is an opportunity to make your communication as effective as your engineering work. We will use real situations, practice alternatives, and review what works. I will be candid and respectful, and I expect you to take ownership of trying new approaches."

## Discovery questions
- What does excellent collaboration look like to you?
- Which communication situations are easiest and hardest?
- What feedback have you heard more than once?
- What happens when you feel misunderstood or challenged?
- What would you like colleagues to say is different after this program?
- How willing are you to experiment with unfamiliar habits?

If the participant does not see a reason to participate, explore the mismatch. Coaching requires willingness; the manager remains responsible for role expectations and accountability.
