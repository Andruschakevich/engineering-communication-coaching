# Session 4 – Structured communication

**Duration:** 75 minutes  
**Primary outcome:** The participant can communicate a technical issue or recommendation concisely, with a clear point, supporting reasoning, and an explicit ask.

## Preparation

Ask the participant to bring a non-confidential technical topic that can be explained to both an engineering peer and a non-technical stakeholder. Prepare a timer.

## Agenda

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Check-in and listening homework |
| 10–22 | Why structure matters |
| 22–37 | Three practical frameworks |
| 37–62 | Timed explanation drills |
| 62–70 | Feedback and second attempt |
| 70–75 | Homework |

## 1. Check-in (0–10)

Ask what changed when the participant listened before responding. Connect listening to structure: understanding the audience's actual question helps determine what information belongs in the answer.

## 2. Why structure matters (10–22)

Explain that structure reduces cognitive load. The listener should not have to guess:
- What is the point?
- Why does it matter?
- What do you recommend?
- What decision or action is needed?

A concise answer is not necessarily a short answer. It is an answer in which each detail serves the listener's need.

## 3. Frameworks (22–37)

### BLUF – Bottom Line Up Front

Start with the conclusion or recommendation, then provide the supporting context.

Example: “I recommend delaying the rollout by one day because the retry behavior is not safe under partial outages. We can complete the validation tomorrow and release with lower operational risk.”

### PREP – Point, Reason, Example, Point

Useful for explaining or defending an opinion:
- Point: state your view.
- Reason: explain why.
- Example: provide evidence.
- Point: restate the conclusion.

### Problem – Impact – Options – Recommendation

Useful for technical and operational decisions:
1. Problem: what is happening?
2. Impact: why does it matter?
3. Options: what choices exist?
4. Recommendation: what should happen next, and why?

Teach the participant to finish with an explicit ask: decision, owner, feedback, or approval.

## 4. Timed drills (37–62)

Use one topic and vary the audience.

**Round A – Engineer (90 seconds):** Explain a production reliability concern with enough technical detail for a peer.

**Round B – Product manager (60 seconds):** Explain user or delivery impact, options, and recommendation without unnecessary implementation detail.

**Round C – Executive (30 seconds):** State the decision needed, business impact, and risk.

**Round D – Unexpected question:** Facilitator interrupts with “What do you recommend?” or “What happens if we do nothing?” Participant answers directly, then adds context.

Score each attempt on:
- Main point appears early.
- Logical sequence.
- Appropriate level of detail.
- Evidence supports the claim.
- Clear recommendation or ask.
- Respectful, confident delivery.

## 5. Feedback and repeat (62–70)

Ask:
- “What did the listener need to know first?”
- “Which detail could be removed?”
- “Was the recommendation explicit?”
Give one improvement point and repeat the weakest round.

## 6. Homework (70–75)

Prepare one real work update in three versions:
- 30 seconds for an executive.
- 1 minute for a product stakeholder.
- 2 minutes for an engineering peer.

Use BLUF or Problem–Impact–Options–Recommendation. Include the decision or action needed.

## Facilitator watch-outs

- Do not reward brevity at the expense of accuracy.
- Avoid forcing a framework into every casual conversation.
- Make sure the participant distinguishes facts, assumptions, and recommendations.
- If the explanation is unclear, ask the participant to identify the audience and desired outcome before rewriting it.

## Completion criteria

The participant can explain the same issue to three audiences at appropriate levels of detail and state a clear recommendation or ask.
