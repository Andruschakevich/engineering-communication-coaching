# Session 2 – Respectful communication and emotional intelligence

**Duration:** 75 minutes  
**Primary outcome:** The participant can express disagreement, frustration, and humor without demeaning people or undermining collaboration.

## Preparation

- Review the participant's chosen diary examples, including positive examples.
- Prepare scenarios involving technical disagreement, a missed commitment, and a joke that lands badly.
- Select one behavior to observe: wording, tone, interruption, labeling, or response to impact.

## Agenda

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Check-in and diary review |
| 10–20 | Intent versus impact |
| 20–35 | Respectful language toolkit |
| 35–60 | Roleplay and repeat practice |
| 60–70 | Debrief and personal rules |
| 70–75 | Homework |

## 1. Check-in (0–10)

Ask:
- “Which interaction went better than expected?”
- “When did you notice an urge to react?”
- “What did you try differently?”
Reinforce effort and specific behavior, not vague praise.

## 2. Intent versus impact (10–20)

Explain: intention matters, but it does not fully determine how a message affects another person. A person can intend humor and still embarrass a colleague; intend efficiency and still sound contemptuous.

Use the distinction:
- **Intent:** what I meant to achieve.
- **Behavior:** what I actually said or did.
- **Impact:** what happened for the other person or the discussion.
- **Repair:** what I can do after noticing the impact.

Ask: “Can you think of a time when your intention and the impact were different?”

## 3. Respectful language toolkit (20–35)

Teach five substitutions:

| Avoid | Try |
|---|---|
| “That's stupid.” | “I see a problem with this assumption.” |
| “You don't understand.” | “Let me explain the part I think we're interpreting differently.” |
| “Obviously…” | “The key point is…” |
| “Did you even test it?” | “Which cases have we validated?” |
| “Calm down.” | “I can see this is frustrating. Can we slow down and examine the issue?” |

Teach **challenge the idea, preserve the person's dignity**:
1. State the concern.
2. Give evidence or reasoning.
3. Explain the risk or consequence.
4. Invite examination or propose a next step.

Discuss humor: humor that depends on a colleague being the target is risky, especially across differences in seniority, culture, language, or relationship. When unsure, do not use it.

## 4. Roleplay (35–60)

Run three rounds. Each round: 2 minutes roleplay, 2 minutes self-assessment, 2 minutes feedback, 2 minutes repeat.

**Scenario A – Technical disagreement**  
A teammate proposes a design you believe will create production risk. Your task: challenge the proposal without judging the person.

**Scenario B – Missed commitment**  
A colleague misses a handoff and your work is blocked. Your task: describe the impact and agree on a recovery plan without sarcasm or blame.

**Scenario C – Joke lands badly**  
A colleague says, “That joke was not okay.” Your task: respond without dismissing their reaction or making them defend it.

Possible repair language:
- “I intended that as a joke, but I can see it did not land well. Sorry.”
- “I did not mean to put you down. I will avoid jokes like that.”
- “Thanks for telling me. Is there something specific I should understand?”

Do not force an apology script if it becomes performative. Ask what accountability would look like in the situation.

## 5. Debrief and personal rules (60–70)

Ask:
- “Which phrase changed the tone most?”
- “What was hardest to avoid?”
- “How can you tell that disagreement is becoming personal?”
- “What will you do when you notice that happening?”

Co-create 2–3 personal rules, for example:
- No labels about intelligence or competence.
- No humor at another person's expense.
- Ask one clarifying question before challenging an idea.

## 6. Homework (70–75)

Rewrite 10 messages or statements:
- Keep the underlying point.
- Remove blame, labels, sarcasm, and unnecessary intensity.
- Make the request or next step explicit.

Use realistic but sanitized examples. Bring three rewrites to the next session.

## Facilitator watch-outs

- Do not equate respectful communication with being agreeable or avoiding directness.
- Do not police personality, accent, or harmless stylistic differences.
- Focus on specific behavior and workplace impact.
- If the participant says “people are too sensitive,” return to the practical question: “What response is most likely to achieve your goal?”

## Completion criteria

The participant can challenge an idea without attacking a person, acknowledge impact, and demonstrate a simple repair response.
